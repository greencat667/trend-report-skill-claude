# Executive Summary

This report is intended to provoke discussion, not settle it.

## Why this matters now

Councils across the UK are funding tool libraries and "library of things" schemes from climate and waste budgets, not waiting for central government money. Right-to-repair law is tightening in the EU and parts of the US, with the UK watching closely. And cost-of-living pressure is pulling people toward borrowing for reasons that have nothing to do with the environment. We assess this as an **emerging priority** — not yet a live operational concern, but the funding and policy window is open now in a way it may not stay.

## What we found

The landscape is dominated by political and legal momentum — council funding and right-to-repair law are moving fastest — while environmental measurement (durability indices, repair-tracking data) is still brand new. Established schemes like Library of Things and Edinburgh Tool Library show real scale: tens of thousands of members, tens of thousands of rentals. But funding is fragile, volunteer numbers are falling nationally, and insurance for power-tool lending remains a genuine gap most small schemes haven't solved.

## Strategic implications

Route this through our Advocacy pillar first: councils are actively seeking local partners to fund, and being at that table early shapes the terms. It also tests Sustainability directly — the biggest open question is whether a hub can run on loan-fee income or always needs a subsidy. Engagement benefits too: borrowing a drill is a low-stakes way to reach people who'd never come to a campaign meeting. Inaction risks watching councils fund whichever partner shows up first, which may not be us.

## Learning priorities for Phase 2

- **Funding beyond the start-up grant** – whether tool libraries can become self-sustaining, or always need ongoing subsidy
- **Who isn't in the room** – whether renters and lower-income households are being reached, or just people already inclined to share
- **What tips a household from buying to borrowing** – the specific moment that changes behaviour, not just general goodwill

We've been surprised before by how fast a "nice idea" scheme can either take off or quietly close. This one's worth watching closely, and worth acting on before the funding window narrows.


# Trend Snapshot

## Definition & scope

A tool library lends equipment the way a book library lends books: members borrow a drill, a carpet cleaner, or a gazebo instead of buying one outright. We're looking at tool libraries, "library of things" schemes, and community equipment shares in the UK and comparable countries — how they're funded, who runs them, and how they connect to the wider repair movement. We're not covering commercial rental platforms or council recycling infrastructure.

Three broad approaches frame today's landscape:

1. **Volunteer-run libraries** — grassroots schemes like Edinburgh Tool Library, often the first of their kind in a city
2. **Licensed platform schemes** — Library of Things now licenses its booking system to councils directly
3. **Council-hosted "Lend and Mend" hubs** — libraries and community buildings repurposed as lending and repair points

## Why now

Three shifts heighten urgency. Councils are funding repair and lending schemes directly from climate and waste budgets, not waiting for central government money. The EU's Right to Repair Directive is pushing manufacturers to open up spare parts and manuals, with the UK watching closely. And cost-of-living pressure is pushing people toward borrowing for reasons that have nothing to do with the environment.

We assess this as an **emerging priority**: not yet a live operational concern, but the funding and policy window is open now in a way it may not stay.

## Relevance to us

This connects most directly to our Advocacy and Sustainability pillars — councils are actively looking for local partners to fund, and self-sustaining models are still unproven. It also touches Engagement: borrowing a drill is a low-stakes, everyday way for people to encounter shared ownership who'd never come to a campaign meeting. The equity lens matters here — renters and lower-income households have the most to gain from not owning things they rarely use, but they're not automatically the people signing up first.


# Top 5 Signals & Drivers

Tool libraries are moving from volunteer side-project to a service councils actively fund and licence.

1. **Councils are the funder of first resort** — UK councils increasingly pay for tool libraries from climate and waste budgets, treating them as core service, not charity extra.
2. **Right to repair is becoming binding law** — the EU and US states are legislating manufacturers must share spare parts and manuals, with the UK watching closely.
3. **Cost of living is doing what campaigning alone couldn't** — people are borrowing for their bills, not just their values, filling appointment slots for reasons beyond the environment.
4. **Booking technology is removing the volunteer bottleneck** — self-service lockers let schemes run in libraries and offices without a rota of people on shift.
5. **Trust in shared ownership is becoming ordinary** — established schemes are logging tens of thousands of members and rentals, turning borrowing into an everyday errand, not a fringe habit.

- A conflicted, transitional future – progress in some neighbourhoods, stalled pilots in others
- A challenging, cautionary future – schemes wither as funding and volunteers dry up

## Scenario A — The Corner Locker

It's a wet Tuesday in Levenshulme, 2039, and Priya is late for work again — not because of the rain, but because Mo from number 14 is still deciding between the pressure washer and the carpet cleaner.

"Take both," Priya says. "Nobody's booked the washer till Thursday."

The lockers sit where the old phone box used to be: waist-high, solar-topped, scuffed at the corners from a decade of drills and stepladders passing through. Priya taps her card, and a green light blinks on unit six. She's not a volunteer any more — the hub pays her twelve hours a week to restock and do basic repairs, funded through the neighbourhood's tool-share levy, a line on the council tax bill nobody objects to any more.

Her son's class is doing a project on "the shed years" — what people did before hubs, when every house on the street owned its own unused ladder. He thinks it's mad. She remembers thinking the opposite: that lending your good drill to a stranger was mad.

Not everything runs smooth. The quarterly meeting last month argued for an hour about whether the pressure washers should be first-come or booked — feelings ran high, because everyone remembers the bank holiday scramble two summers back. Priya voted for booking. She lost.

She waves Mo off with the trolley and checks the board: two ladders back late, one strimmer flagged for a blade replacement. Ordinary problems, for an ordinary Tuesday. She locks up at five and walks home past a shed that hasn't held a single tool in six years — her neighbour uses it to grow tomatoes now.

# Second-Order Effects

Most analysis of tool libraries focuses on the direct effect: fewer things bought, fewer things wasted. But the changes that matter most for our strategy come one step further out — the knock-on consequences nobody planned for.

**Manufacturers stop designing for a single sale.** Fewer individual purchases means fewer individual buyers. Manufacturers who sell into hubs instead of households face a different customer: one who cares about ten-year durability and spare-parts availability, not just shelf appeal. Product design shifts toward the kind of heavy-duty, repairable kit hubs actually want to stock.

**A niche insurance market grows up around lending.** Volunteer schemes currently struggle to find cover for power tools at all. As hubs multiply and claims data builds, insurers get enough evidence to price the risk properly. That's good for hubs — but it also means insurance becomes a real cost line, not a volunteer favour, and schemes without a paying model may get priced out.

**Repair becomes a recognised local livelihood, not just a hobby.** Repair cafés and hub workshops currently run on volunteers. As demand grows, some of that work turns into paid part-time roles — fixing kit, training members, managing bookings. A skill that used to live in one person's shed becomes a small, local repair economy.

**Freed-up storage space gets repurposed.** Sheds and garages that once held a single-use pressure washer or ladder empty out. Some becomes home offices, some becomes growing space, some gets built over entirely as gardens densify. A shift that starts with tools ends up quietly changing what a back garden is for.

## So what for us

The insurance shift is the one to watch first — it could lock out exactly the small, volunteer-run hubs we'd want to support unless we help them access cover collectively. The manufacturer shift is a genuine opportunity: if durable, repairable kit becomes standard, our advocacy work gets easier, not harder. The local-livelihood effect is worth preparing for now, since it changes who we're funding — not just volunteers, but small local employers.


# Key Open Questions

These are learning priorities for our programme staff to explore in Phase 2.

## Learning Priority 1 — Funding beyond the start-up grant

- **Which councils have moved a tool library from grant-funded to self-sustaining, and how?** — Is loan-fee income realistic, or does every scheme need an ongoing subsidy?
- **What would a shared insurance or back-office arrangement across several small hubs cost, versus each negotiating cover alone?**

*Why it matters:* Directly tests our Sustainability pillar. If we back a hub with a start-up grant and it folds when the grant ends, we've spent money on a pilot, not a programme.

## Learning Priority 2 — Who isn't in the room

- **Which households in our area are least likely to know a tool library exists, and why?** — Is it awareness, trust, transport, or something else?
- **Do renters use tool libraries differently from homeowners, given renters have less storage and shorter tenure?**

*Why it matters:* Connects to our Engagement pillar. A scheme that only reaches people already inclined to share isn't changing behaviour — it's serving people who'd have found a way anyway.

## Learning Priority 3 — What actually tips a household from buying to borrowing

- **What's the specific moment someone chooses a hub over a shop — price, convenience, or something social?**
- **Does one good experience create repeat use, or does each borrow require re-persuading?**

*Why it matters:* This is our Advocacy and Impact case in miniature. If we can name the tipping point precisely, we can make a sharper ask to councils and funders than "people like it."

## Macro-drivers (PESTEL)

**Political**

- **Councils fund repair infrastructure directly (H2)** — UK councils increasingly pay for tool libraries from climate and waste budgets, not central government grants. - **Cost-of-living pressure pushes people to borrow, not buy (H2)** — Scotland's library-based "Lend and Mend" hubs were funded partly to help households facing high bills. **Social**

- **Trust in shared ownership is normalising (H2)** — Library of Things has signed up almost 40,000 members and logged over 70,000 rentals across 22 UK locations. **Technological**

- **Digital lockers remove the volunteer bottleneck (H2)** — Library of Things builds self-service lockers so people can book and collect items with no volunteer on shift. **Environmental**

- **Product lifetimes become a measured policy target (H2)** — France now scores appliances on a durability index, covering TVs since January 2025 and washing machines since April 2025. **Legal**

- **Manufacturers must open up parts and manuals (H2)** — The EU's Right to Repair Directive, in force since July 2024, requires manufacturers to make spare parts, tools and repair information available. ## Signals & drivers analysis

Political and legal drivers dominate: right-to-repair law and council funding are moving fastest. Technology is following, not leading. Environmental measurement is thin — durability indices are brand new. Our best foothold is council climate budgets and library buildings, not national policy.


# Appendix 2: Systems Context

## Why we map the system

We map the system because tool libraries don't grow in isolation. A **reinforcing loop** is a cycle that amplifies a trend — more borrowers build more trust, which attracts more donors and volunteers. A **balancing loop** resists change — a bad insurance claim can quietly shrink an inventory. Spotting these loops helps us find where a small push creates a big shift.

## Scope statement

We cover UK (and comparable) tool libraries, library-of-things schemes, and equipment shares: their funding, governance, and links to repair culture. We exclude commercial rental platforms and formal waste-disposal infrastructure.

## Key actors

**Institutions & regulators**: Local councils, several of which now co-fund sharing libraries, such as Southwark's new sites in East Dulwich and Elephant and Castle ([Southwark Council](https://www.southwark.gov.uk/news/2025/new-sharing-libraries-east-dulwich-and-elephant-and-castle)), and the City of London Corporation ([City of London](https://www.fis.cityoflondon.gov.uk/news/city-of-london-library-of-things)).

**Private sector**: Tool manufacturers and retailers whose business model assumes individual ownership; insurers who price public liability cover for lending schemes.

**Civic groups**: Library of Things ([libraryofthings.co.uk](https://www.libraryofthings.co.uk/)), Edinburgh Tool Library, the UK's first ([Edinburgh Tool Library](https://edinburghtoollibrary.org.uk/)), and the Restart Project, which runs repair events under its own public liability cover ([The Restart Project](https://therestartproject.org/annual-reports/2023-annual-report/)).

**International bodies**: The Ellen MacArthur Foundation frames tool sharing as a potential public utility ([Ellen MacArthur Foundation](https://www.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/circular-examples/how-tool-sharing-could-become-a-public-utility)). The EU's Right to Repair Directive sets a regional direction of travel that UK policy watches closely ([Council of the EU](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/policies/right-to-repair-products/)).

**Communities**: Volunteers, borrowers, and networks like Scotland's Share and Repair Network, backed by Zero Waste Scotland funding ([Friends of the Earth Scotland](https://foe.scot/how-edinburgh-tool-library-is-helping-to-reduce-consumption/)).

## Relationships and feedback loops

Council funding and venue space let libraries open; usage data then helps councils justify renewal, a reinforcing loop. But most council grants run in short annual cycles. That creates a balancing loop: staff spend time chasing the next grant instead of growing membership. Retailers have little direct stake either way, since borrowing mostly reaches people who wouldn't have bought new. Insurers sit in the middle. As claims data builds, cover should get easier to price, which reinforces growth. But a single bad incident can spook a scheme's committee into shrinking its offer.

## Tensions and collaborations

Volunteer burnout is the sharpest tension. Most libraries run on a small core team, and losing two or three people can close a site. Insurance and liability concerns push some schemes toward narrower tool ranges, in tension with an open-access ethos. Retail interests rarely oppose libraries outright, but neither do they fund them. Collaboration is real. Edinburgh Tool Library actively advises new sharing libraries, including Oxford's and Plymouth's Borrow Don't Buy ([Edinburgh Tool Library](https://edinburghtoollibrary.org.uk/advice-for-new-sharing-libraries/)). The Restart Project coordinates with local councils on repair events too. But this peer support runs on goodwill, not funding, which limits how far it scales.

## Scenario C — The Quiet Shelf

Aaliyah drives past the old library building most days now. The windows are papered over from the inside; the sign still says "Borrow, Don't Buy," faded to nearly nothing.

# Appendix 4: Detailed SWOT Analysis

## Strengths
**Deep volunteer relationships** – We've worked with local volunteer groups for years. That's a head start on recruiting tool-library stewards, instead of building a volunteer base from zero.

**A working relationship with the council** – We already sit at the table on local environmental policy. Other councils already host tool libraries in partnership with local charities, as Enfield Council does at Palmers Green Library ([Enfield Independent](https://www.enfieldindependent.co.uk/news/25193351.enfield-tool-library-open-palmers-green-library/)). We can make a similar case using contacts we already have.

**Mission fit** – Lending tools instead of buying new ones cuts waste and carbon. That's a direct link to our purpose, not a side project.

**Some funding diversity already** – We already mix grants, donations, and small membership income for other programmes. We're not starting our funding model from a blank page.

## Opportunities
**A genuinely growing sector** – The global sharing economy is forecast to grow from around $245 billion in 2025 to over $300 billion in 2026 ([ResearchAndMarkets](https://www.researchandmarkets.com/reports/5989696/sharing-economy-market-report)). We can ride that wave rather than pitch something unproven.

**Right-to-repair momentum** – "Right to repair" is the campaign for easier, cheaper repair of everyday goods. Nearly 400 community groups and 35 MPs now back the Repair & Reuse Declaration ([The Restart Project](https://therestartproject.org/right-to-repair/uk-progress-2025/)). A tool library gives us a local, concrete way to join that campaign.

**Council funding routes exist** – Councils can draw on climate, sustainability, and waste budgets, or the Community Infrastructure Levy (a charge on new developments), to back schemes like ours ([Library of Things](https://participate.libraryofthings.co.uk/licensing-councils)). We can make that ask locally.

**A ready toolkit** – Library of Things and Share Shed have published free setup guides ([Shareable](https://www.shareable.net/toolkit/library-of-things/)). We don't need to design this from scratch.

## Weaknesses
**No in-house logistics or retail skills** – We've never run a lending operation, booking system, or physical stock. This is our biggest gap, and it's a real one.

**Thin staff capacity** – We're a few dozen staff already stretched across campaigns. A tool library needs someone answering bookings, checking returns, and fixing broken kit – time we don't have spare.

**Volunteer-only opening hours** – Similar schemes often open just once or twice a week because they run on volunteer rotas. The Edinburgh Tool Library does exactly this at each of its hubs ([Edinburgh Tool Library](https://edinburghtoollibrary.org.uk/faq/)). We'd likely face the same limit.

**Low risk appetite** – We're cautious by habit. Lending power tools to the public is a bigger liability step than anything we do now.

## Threats
**Funding is genuinely fragile** – Nearly half of small UK charities fear closure within a year ([Civil Society](https://www.civilsociety.co.uk/news/nearly-half-of-small-charities-fear-risk-of-closure-within-a-year-research-says.html)). A lost grant or council contract could stall us fast.

**Volunteer numbers are falling** – Monthly formal volunteering in England has dropped from 23% of adults in 2019-20 to 16% in 2023-24 ([Civil Society](https://www.civilsociety.co.uk/news/volunteering-rates-still-failing-to-reach-pre-pandemic-levels-government-reports.html)). Relying on volunteer stewards risks burning out the few we have.

**Insurance gaps around power tools** – Standard volunteer liability cover often excludes power-tool use ([Insureon](https://www.insureon.com/blog/does-nonprofit-insurance-protect-your-volunteers)). An injury claim could cost more than the scheme itself.

**Political turnover at council level** – Election results can swing council priorities overnight, taking partnership funding with them. A supportive council today may not back us next year.


# Appendix 5: Three Horizons Exploration

The Three Horizons framework maps change without treating it as a straight timeline. All three horizons exist together, right now. Horizon 1 (H1) is the system running most of daily life today. Horizon 2 (H2) is the alternative already testing that system's edges. Horizon 3 (H3) is the seed of something genuinely different, already growing underneath.

## Horizon 1 — The dominant system

Today, if you need a drill, you buy a drill. Individual private ownership is the default. Retailers and manufacturers hold the power. They design products, set prices, and decide how long things last. The model is stable because it is simple: no booking, no returning, no waiting your turn. UK shoppers spend heavily on tools they own outright — the power tools market here is worth around $1.3 billion a year ([IMARC Group](https://www.imarcgroup.com/united-kingdom-power-tools-market)). But cracks are showing. Many tools sit unused most of the year, filling sheds and garages. Cost pressures make one-off purchases harder to justify: around a quarter of UK adults say they could not cover an unexpected £850 expense ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/wellbeing/bulletins/publicopinionsandsocialtrendsgreatbritain/july2024)). Right-to-repair rules are tightening, but slowly ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-9302/)).

## Horizon 2 — Transitional tensions

Across the UK, tool libraries and "library of things" schemes are testing an alternative: borrowing instead of buying. Library of Things now runs 22 locations, with nearly 40,000 members and almost 60,000 rentals to date ([Cooperative City](https://cooperativecity.org/inside-the-library-of-things-uk/)). Members borrow drills, carpet cleaners, and camping gear instead of owning them outright. Friction shows up fast. Early volunteer-run schemes struggled with poor-quality donated items and volunteers who moved on ([Cooperative City](https://cooperativecity.org/inside-the-library-of-things-uk/)). Councils fund much of the start-up cost, through carbon-offset budgets or National Lottery grants, but few schemes yet cover running costs from loan fees alone. That is the tipping point: access has to compete with ownership on convenience, not only on ethics. Competing logics collide inside manufacturers too. One library founder describes sales teams pushed to sell more product, while sustainability teams in the same company push toward repair and reuse ([Cooperative City](https://cooperativecity.org/inside-the-library-of-things-uk/)). Right-to-repair rules cover some appliances but exclude much DIY and community repair from professional-grade parts access ([Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/state-of-disrepair-are-our-right-to-repair-laws-broken-alpGt1I7sZL8)). Repair cafés, now numbering over 2,500 worldwide, show that demand for fixing things exists ([Repair Café International](https://www.repaircafe.org/en/about/)). The supply chain to support it does not, yet.

## Horizon 3 — Visionary future

Picture a terraced street on a Saturday morning. Nobody on it owns a pressure washer. Everybody has access to one, booked on a phone, collected from a locker at the end of the road. This is not a special event. It is an ordinary Tuesday, too.

Ownership has not disappeared. It has become selective. People still own what they use daily: their phone, their bike, their kettle. But things used only a few times a year are different. The strimmer, the carpet cleaner, the gazebo, the good ladder — these sit in shared local hubs instead of a thousand separate sheds. One well-built tool, borrowed by forty households a year, replaces forty tools gathering dust. Manufacturers build for this now: heavier-duty parts, modular repairs, ten-year spare-parts guarantees as standard, not a regulatory afterthought.

Power has shifted too. Decisions about what a hub stocks are made by the people who use it, at open quarterly meetings, not by a distant retailer's stock algorithm. Many hubs run on a "steward-owned" model. A community trust holds a guardian share, with a veto over any decision that puts profit ahead of people or planet. Today's Library of Things already pilots this, in embryonic form. Riverside Environmental Trust runs three hubs directly. We back a dozen more with start-up grants, insurance templates, and a shared booking platform. It is the same behind-the-scenes role a housing association plays for a tenants' co-op.

The old harms have not vanished, but they have shrunk. Overconsumption falls, because checking the hub, not buying, is the default first move. Cost-of-living pressure eases, because a big one-off purchase becomes a small booking fee. Waste drops, because tools are built, and paid for, to last decades, not years.

It is not utopia. Waiting lists form for popular kit on bank-holiday weekends. Some people still miss the instant gratification of owning outright. Rural hubs face delivery costs that dense cities do not. Debate continues over charging and who gets priority when demand spikes. But borrowing has become the obvious first thought, not the earnest exception. That shift traces back to seeds planted today: repair cafés, mission-locked companies, and councils willing to fund a locker before anyone knew it would work.

## Getting from here to there

Nothing here moves in a straight line. We could stay stuck in H2, with tool libraries as worthy add-ons rather than the default. Reaching H3 depends on choices made now. Will right-to-repair law close its DIY loophole? Will councils fund hubs long enough to break even? Will steward-owned models spread beyond one or two pioneers? We need to keep backing local hubs with grants and expertise. We should push for stronger repair rules, too. And we can help hubs learn from each other faster than manufacturers can out-market them.
