# Second-Order Effects

> **Section 5** | Target: ~300 words (±5%) | Pause for review after drafting

## Prompt

Draft **Section 5: Second-Order Effects** (~300 words) for the report on **{{TOPIC}}**.

This section answers the question: **what happens after the obvious transition?** The earlier sections describe the trend itself — cost changes, policy shifts, deployment patterns. This section looks at what those changes set in motion over 10-15 years. These are consequences that are not yet visible in the data but follow logically from the trajectory.

### Structure

1. **Framing paragraph** (≤50 words) — Explain that most analysis focuses on the direct effects of the trend. But the changes that matter most for strategy often come one step further out — the knock-on consequences that reshape industries, communities, and politics in ways the trend's champions didn't plan for.

2. **4-5 second-order effects** (≤50 words each) — Each should:
   - Have a bold heading that names the effect
   - Explain the causal chain: [direct trend] → [first-order consequence] → [second-order effect]
   - Be specific enough to be useful for strategic planning — not "everything changes" but "this specific thing changes because of that"
   - Where possible, name who is affected and how

3. **So what for us** (≤50 words) — A brief paragraph connecting the second-order effects to {{ORG}}'s strategic position. Which of these effects should we be preparing for? Which create opportunities? Which could blindside us?

### What counts as a second-order effect?

- Not the trend itself (that's Section 1)
- Not the direct drivers (that's Section 2 / Appendix 1)
- But the consequences of consequences: what happens to labour markets, land use, geopolitics, cultural norms, inequality patterns, or institutional legitimacy *because of* the changes described elsewhere in the report
- Think: "If this trend succeeds beyond expectations, what breaks? What gets created that nobody asked for?"

## Style Rules

- Write as a colleague, not a consultant. First person plural ("we", "our").
- Plain, direct English. Short sentences (target under 15 words). Break long sentences in two.
- Speculative but grounded — each effect should follow logically from evidence in earlier sections
- Name the causal chain explicitly, don't just assert the outcome
- This is the section where longer-range strategic imagination earns its keep — reward the reader for getting this far
- Avoid consultant-speak: don't write "systemic implications", "cascading impacts", "paradigm disruption". Name the actual consequence and who it hits. Before/after example: ❌ "Labour market disruption creates cascading socioeconomic implications" → ✅ "Factory workers lose their jobs. The towns that depend on those factories lose their tax base. Schools close."
- Define technical terms briefly on first use — assume the reader is smart but not a specialist
- **No meta-text:** Do not include word counts, drafting notes, or other artefacts in your output.
